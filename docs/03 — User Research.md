# 1. Purpose

This document defines how user research will be conducted for the Stock Management SaaS.

The purpose is to understand:

- Who experiences stock-management problems
- How businesses currently manage stock
- What happens when things go wrong
- How frequently problems occur
- What businesses currently use
- What workarounds they have created
- What existing software they use
- What they dislike about current solutions
- What problems have financial or operational consequences
- Which problems are worth solving first

The objective is **not to convince people to use the product**.

The objective is to discover whether the problems we believe exist actually occur in real businesses.

---

# 2. Research Principle

The research process should focus on **real behavior rather than hypothetical opinions**.

Prefer:

> "Tell me about the last time your stock didn't match."

Instead of:

> "Would you use an app that detects stock discrepancies?"

Prefer:

> "How do you currently know when to reorder?"

Instead of:

> "Would automatic reorder alerts be useful?"

Recent real experiences provide stronger evidence than hypothetical feature preferences.

---

# 3. Research Goals

The initial research round has six major goals.

## Goal 1 — Understand the Business

Understand:

- Type of business
- Approximate size
- Products handled
- Number of people involved in stock
- How frequently stock changes
- How stock is currently recorded

---

## Goal 2 — Understand the Current Workflow

Document how stock moves through the business.

Example:

```text
Supplier
   ↓
Purchase
   ↓
Delivery
   ↓
Stock Received
   ↓
Storage/Shelf
   ↓
Customer Sale
   ↓
Stock Reduced
   ↓
Physical Count
```

The research should identify where problems occur within this workflow.

---

## Goal 3 — Identify Painful Problems

Find out:

- What goes wrong
- How often it happens
- Why it happens
- What the business does about it
- What happens if nothing is done

---

## Goal 4 — Understand Existing Solutions

Determine whether the business uses:

- Notebook
- Excel
- Google Sheets
- POS
- Inventory software
- Accounting software
- Multiple systems
- Manual processes
- A combination of tools

---

## Goal 5 — Understand Unmet Needs

Identify:

- Missing functionality
- Difficult workflows
- Unnecessary complexity
- Expensive software
- Poor support
- Connectivity problems
- Data-entry problems
- Reporting problems
- Stock investigation problems

---

## Goal 6 — Identify the Strongest Initial Customer Segment

We should eventually be able to answer:

> "Which type of business has the strongest and most frequent problem that we can realistically solve?"

# 5. Participant Selection

Research should not consist only of people who already like technology.

Include a mixture of:

- Businesses using notebooks
- Businesses using spreadsheets
- Businesses using POS systems
- Businesses using inventory software
- Businesses using multiple systems
- Businesses with very small teams
- Businesses with several employees

This prevents the product from being designed around only one type of user.

---

# 6. Initial Research Sample

The first round should aim to collect enough interviews to identify recurring patterns.

Target:

**5–8 interviews per major research question or customer segment.**

A small number of interviews can reveal useful patterns, but repeated evidence is important before making major product decisions.

Initial target:

```text
10–20 meaningful business conversations
```

This can include:

- LinkedIn responses
- Direct messages
- Phone conversations
- In-person conversations
- Video calls
- Business visits where appropriate

# 7. Research Channels

## LinkedIn

Use the existing research post to collect:

- Problem categories
- Business types
- Common complaints
- People willing to continue the conversation

---

## Direct Messages

When someone gives an interesting response, ask for a deeper conversation.

The goal is to move from:

> "Missing stock"

to:

> "Tell me about the last time that happened."

---

## In-Person Research

Where possible, observe businesses managing stock.

Observation can reveal things people forget to mention during interviews.

Look for:

- Notebooks
- Receipts
- Stock cards
- Spreadsheets
- Phones
- POS terminals
- Physical counting
- Repeated manual calculations
- Multiple systems

---

# 8. Research Introduction

Start each interview with a neutral explanation.

Example:

> I'm researching how businesses currently manage stock and where the process becomes difficult. I'm not selling anything, and I'm not trying to convince you to use a particular system. I'd like to understand how you currently work and what problems you actually experience.

The researcher should avoid presenting the product before understanding the participant's current workflow.

---

# 9. Participant Context Questions

Start with basic context.

### Questions

1. What type of business do you operate?
2. What products do you normally keep in stock?
3. Approximately how many different products do you manage?
4. How many people are involved in handling stock?
5. Who is responsible for checking stock?
6. Who records sales?
7. Who receives new stock?

These questions establish the environment without immediately steering the conversation toward a particular solution.

---

# 10. Current Workflow Questions

Ask the participant to describe what they actually do.

### Questions

1. How do you currently track your stock?
2. Walk me through what happens when new stock arrives.
3. How do you record a sale?
4. How does the stock quantity change after a sale?
5. How do you perform a physical stock count?
6. How do you compare physical stock with recorded stock?
7. Who checks whether the numbers are correct?
8. What happens when the numbers don't match?

---

# 11. Missing Stock Questions

If the participant mentions missing stock, investigate a real example.

### Questions

1. Tell me about the last time stock was missing.
2. What product was involved?
3. How did you discover the problem?
4. What did your records show?
5. What did the physical count show?
6. How large was the difference?
7. What did you do next?
8. Did you find the cause?
9. How long did the investigation take?
10. Did the problem cost the business money?

---

# 12. Reordering Questions

### Questions

1. How do you know when a product needs to be reordered?
2. Who makes the decision?
3. What usually triggers a reorder?
4. Tell me about the last time you ran out of an important product.
5. What happened?
6. How long did it take to replace the stock?
7. Did you lose any sales?
8. How do you decide how much to order?

---

# 13. Slow-Moving Stock Questions

### Questions

1. How do you know when a product isn't selling?
2. Can you remember the last product that sat in stock for too long?
3. How long did it remain unsold?
4. How did you eventually discover it?
5. What did you do with it?
6. Did the business lose money because of it?
7. Do you currently have products you suspect are not moving?

---

# 14. Sales and Stock Matching Questions

### Questions

1. How do you currently compare sales with stock?
2. How often do you check whether the numbers match?
3. What normally causes differences?
4. How do you correct them?
5. Who is allowed to make adjustments?
6. Do you keep a record of adjustments?
7. Can you see who made a change?

---

# 15. Staff Accountability Questions

### Questions

1. How many people can change stock records?
2. Can you tell who sold a product?
3. Can you tell who received stock?
4. Can staff make stock adjustments?
5. What happens when an error is discovered?
6. Have you ever had difficulty determining who made a change?
7. How important is knowing who performed an action?

Do not assume that staff accountability is important.

Measure the participant's actual experience.

---

# 16. Customer Credit Questions

Only explore this topic if relevant to the business.

### Questions

1. Do you sell products on credit?
2. How do you record customer debt?
3. How do you know who owes money?
4. How do you follow up?
5. Have you ever lost track of a customer's balance?
6. What happens when payment is delayed?

---

# 17. Current Software Questions

If the participant uses software, investigate it carefully.

### Questions

1. What system do you currently use?
2. How long have you used it?
3. Why did you choose it?
4. What do you use it for?
5. What works well?
6. What frustrates you?
7. What takes too many steps?
8. What do you still do outside the system?
9. What do you use a notebook or spreadsheet for?
10. Have you ever considered changing systems?
11. What stopped you?

These questions help identify the real competition, which may be another application, spreadsheets, notebooks, or a combination of workarounds.

---

# 18. Cost and Value Questions

Avoid starting with:

> "How much would you pay for my app?"

Instead, understand the existing economics.

### Questions

1. Do you currently pay for stock-management software?
2. What does it cost?
3. What else do you pay for to manage stock?
4. What problem does the current system save you from?
5. What happens when the system doesn't work?
6. Have you ever stopped using a system because of cost?
7. What makes software worth paying for?

---

# 19. Connectivity Questions

### Questions

1. How reliable is internet connectivity where you operate?
2. What happens when the connection goes down?
3. Can you continue recording sales?
4. What do you do when the internet is unavailable?
5. Have you ever lost or delayed a transaction because of connectivity?
6. How long can the business operate before connectivity becomes a problem?

This determines whether offline functionality is actually important to the target segment.

---

# 20. Workaround Questions

Workarounds are especially valuable research evidence.

Ask:

> "When your current system cannot do something you need, what do you do instead?"

Follow up with:

- Do you use paper?
- Do you use Excel?
- Do you use WhatsApp?
- Do you use a calculator?
- Do you keep a separate notebook?
- Do you ask another employee?
- Do you manually reconcile the numbers?

A workaround can indicate an unmet need.

---

# 21. Consequence Questions

For every significant problem, investigate its consequence.

Ask:

### Time

> How much time does this normally take?

### Money

> Does this cost the business money?

### Frequency

> How often does this happen?

### Risk

> What happens if you don't fix it?

### Emotional impact

> How frustrating is this when it happens?

### Business impact

> Does it affect customers, staff or sales?

This prevents us from treating every complaint as equally important.

---

# 22. Follow-Up Technique

Do not immediately move to the next question when someone gives an interesting answer.

Use:

> "Tell me more about that."

Then:

> "What happened next?"

Then:

> "Why was that difficult?"

Then:

> "How do you handle it today?"

Then:

> "How often does this happen?"

These follow-ups help uncover the actual workflow behind a short answer.

---

# 23. Questions to Avoid

Avoid leading questions.

### Avoid

> Would automatic stock alerts help you?

### Better

> How do you currently know when stock is getting low?

---

### Avoid

> Would an offline app solve your problem?

### Better

> What happens when your internet connection goes down?

---

### Avoid

> Would you pay KES 500 per month?

### Better

> What do you currently pay for software or other stock-management methods?

---

### Avoid

> Do you think our dashboard is useful?

### Better

> What would you normally want to know first when you open your stock system?

Open, neutral questions are generally better for uncovering genuine problems than questions that suggest the desired answer.

# 24. Research Session Structure

A typical interview can follow:

```text
0–3 min
Introduction

3–8 min
Business context

8–18 min
Current workflow

18–28 min
Problems and recent examples

28–35 min
Existing tools and workarounds

35–40 min
Final observations
```

The exact duration can change depending on the participant.

The interviewer should spend more time listening than explaining the product.

# 27. Participant Privacy

Only collect information necessary for the research.

Avoid recording unnecessary:

- Personal identification information
- Financial account information
- Passwords
- Private business credentials
- Customer personal information

When publishing research findings, remove identifying information unless explicit permission has been provided.

# 28. Evidence Classification

Every finding should receive an evidence status.

## Confirmed

Supported by repeated evidence from multiple participants or reliable evidence sources.

Example:

```text
6 of 10 businesses reported difficulty
identifying stock discrepancies.

Status: CONFIRMED
```

---

## Observed

Directly observed but not yet repeated enough to become a strong pattern.

```text
One shop maintains a notebook alongside
its POS system.

Status: OBSERVED
```

---

## Hypothesis

An interpretation that has not yet been sufficiently tested.

```text
Businesses may value staff-level stock
accountability.

Status: HYPOTHESIS
```

---

## Rejected

Research contradicts the assumption.

```text
Most interviewed businesses do not consider
offline functionality important.

Status: REJECTED AS PRIMARY NEED
```

A rejected assumption is useful information.

# 32. Research Synthesis

After each research round:

1. Review all interviews.
2. Extract repeated problems.
3. Group similar problems.
4. Identify contradictions.
5. Record important quotes.
6. Identify workarounds.
7. Score the problems.
8. Compare business segments.
9. Update assumptions.
10. Decide what requires further research.

Research should lead to a decision, not simply a collection of notes.

A useful discovery workflow is:

```text
Learn
  ↓
Prepare
  ↓
Interview
  ↓
Synthesize
  ↓
Decide
```

The decision may be to build, modify, investigate further, or reject an assumption.

---

# 33. Research Decision Rules

Do not make a major product decision based on:

- One person's opinion
- A single LinkedIn comment
- A hypothetical feature request
- A competitor's marketing claim
- A developer assumption

Look for repeated evidence.

Example:

```text
1 person:
"I sometimes have stock discrepancies."

Result:
Interesting signal.

5 people:
"We regularly struggle to explain stock differences."

Result:
Strong signal.

10+ businesses:
"Stock discrepancies cause financial loss and
take time to investigate."

Result:
Potential core problem.
```

These thresholds are guidelines rather than rigid statistical rules.

# 34. Research-to-Product Pipeline

Research findings should flow into product decisions.

```text
User Conversation
       ↓
Research Record
       ↓
Problem
       ↓
Evidence
       ↓
Problem Score
       ↓
Validated Need
       ↓
Product Requirement
       ↓
Feature
       ↓
Prototype
       ↓
Real-World Test
       ↓
New Research
```

This creates a continuous learning loop.

---

# 35. Example

Suppose five shop owners independently report:

> "I only discover missing stock when I physically count everything."

Research interpretation:

```text
Problem:
Stock discrepancies are discovered late.

Frequency:
High

Impact:
Potentially high

Current workaround:
Manual stock counting.

Opportunity:
Make discrepancies easier to detect and investigate.
```

Possible product requirement:

```text
The system should record stock movements
and allow users to compare expected stock
with physical stock counts.
```

Possible feature:

```text
Inventory → Stock Count → Discrepancy Detection
```

This is a stronger path from research to software than simply adding a feature because it sounds useful.

# 39. Research Success Criteria

The research phase should eventually allow us to state:

```text
Our primary customer is:
[Specific business segment]

Their biggest recurring problem is:
[Specific problem]

It happens:
[Frequency]

It causes:
[Business consequence]

They currently solve it using:
[Current solution]

Existing solutions fail because:
[Specific weakness]

They consider the problem important because:
[Evidence]

Our V1 will solve it by:
[Product approach]
```

If we cannot fill these statements confidently, research is not finished.

---

# 40. Research Guiding Principle

> **Listen for what people actually do, not what they think they might do.**

The purpose of user research is not to prove that the product idea is correct.

The purpose is to discover whether it is correct — and to change it when the evidence says otherwise.
